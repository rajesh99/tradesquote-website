**Above 80 psi static, the IPC requires a pressure reducing valve.** That part is simple and there is a section number for it — 604.8, which is more than can be said for a lot of plumbing rules.

The part nobody mentions when they quote it: **fitting a PRV turns your house into a closed system**, and a closed system requires thermal expansion control. One requirement drags in a second one, and where you set the valve decides how big that second thing has to be.

## The threshold, and why it is where it is

  title="Above 80 psi the extra pressure buys you nothing"

>
  <PrvThresholdDiagram />
</BlogInfographic>

Work the budget at each pressure and the case makes itself:

- **50 psi** — 16.2 psi left for friction
- **65 psi** — **31.2 psi** — comfortable for a long run in reasonable pipe
- **80 psi** — 46.2 psi — generous
- **100 psi** — 66.2 psi, **and a PRV is required**

By 65 psi you already have more friction budget than a normal house needs. Everything above that is not improving anything at the fixtures — it is working every solder joint, every washing machine hose, every appliance inlet valve and every toilet fill valve, permanently.

That is what the threshold is protecting. Not comfort — component life. The pressure band and where it comes from is in **[normal water pressure for a house](/blog/normal-water-pressure-for-a-house)**.

## The requirement it brings with it

<BlogInfographic
  title="A PRV never arrives alone"
  caption="Fitting a valve closes the system, so IPC 607.3 then requires expansion control. And the setting you choose decides the tank: 50 psi needs 2.70 gallons, 70 psi needs 8.10."
>
  <PrvExpansionDiagram />
</BlogInfographic>

A PRV does not let water flow backwards. Before it was fitted, thermal expansion from the water heater pushed harmlessly back toward the street main. Afterwards it has nowhere to go, and pressure climbs on every heating cycle until the T&P valve lets go.

**The classic symptom is a relief valve that starts weeping shortly after a PRV goes in.** The valve is not faulty. It is doing exactly its job, on a system that has just lost its expansion path.

Now look at what the setting does. Against an 80 psi ceiling on a 50 gallon system:

- **PRV at 50 psi** — 31.7% of the shell usable — **2.70 gallon tank**
- **PRV at 60 psi** — 21.1% usable — **4.05 gallons**
- **PRV at 70 psi** — **10.6% usable — 8.10 gallons**

Setting the valve at 70 rather than 50 **triples the required expansion tank**, because you have narrowed the window the tank's air charge can be squeezed through. The full argument is in **[thermal expansion tank sizing](/blog/thermal-expansion-tank-sizing)**, and the **[Water Pressure Calculator](/calculators/plumbing/plumbing-water-pressure-calculator)** works the pressure side.

Note also that a PRV is not the only thing that closes a system. A backflow preventer does it, and so does a **check valve in the meter assembly** — which many utilities now fit as routine, which is how a great many houses became closed systems without their owners being told.

## When it is right, and when it is the wrong tool

<BlogInfographic
  title="Required, useful, or the wrong tool entirely"
  caption="Six situations. The bottom two are complaints a PRV cannot fix and can make worse — and they are the two it gets fitted for most often by mistake."
>
  <PrvDecisionDiagram />
</BlogInfographic>

**Required** above 80 psi static. No judgement involved.

**Worth fitting** between about 70 and 80 psi if appliance warranties matter — many cap inlet pressure below the code threshold — or where street pressure swings widely through the day, since holding a steady downstream pressure is the valve's other genuine function.

**Helpful** against water hammer, because a lower pressure means a lower velocity and a smaller surge. Arrestors do more, but it is a real secondary benefit.

**The wrong tool** for weak flow. If static pressure is healthy and flowing pressure collapses, the fault is friction and a PRV makes it worse by reducing what you started with. That diagnosis is in **[low water pressure in a house](/blog/low-water-pressure-in-house)**.

**The wrong tool** for one weak fixture. Nothing at the meter changes a single fixture — that is an aerator, a cartridge or a supply stop.

## Practical points

**Set it and verify it.** A PRV is adjustable and it does not arrive at your intended setting. Adjust with the system static, then re-read.

**60 psi is a sensible default.** Comfortable at the fixtures, generous on friction budget, and it keeps the expansion tank at a stocked 4.4 gallon shell rather than pushing to the next size.

**They fail, usually low.** A PRV is a spring and a diaphragm, typically good for ten to twenty years. Gradual whole-house pressure loss with no other explanation is a failing PRV before it is anything else.

**Fit a gauge downstream.** The cheapest diagnostic in the house, and it turns "the pressure seems low" into a number.

**Some need a bypass.** Where the PRV serves a fire line or a critical supply, check whether the jurisdiction wants an arrangement that survives the valve failing.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need a pressure reducing valve?

If static pressure exceeds **80 psi**, yes — IPC 604.8 requires one and it is not discretionary. Between 70 and 80 it is often worth fitting anyway, because many appliance and water heater warranties cap inlet pressure below the code threshold.

### What pressure should a PRV be set to?

**60 psi is a sensible default.** It is comfortable at fixtures, leaves about 31 psi of friction budget on a typical two-storey house, and keeps the required expansion tank on the smaller stocked shell. Going to 70 gives you very little more and triples the expansion tank.

### Does a PRV require an expansion tank?

In effect, yes. A PRV stops water flowing back toward the main, which makes the house a **closed system**, and IPC 607.3 requires thermal expansion control on closed systems. Fitting a PRV without one is why relief valves start weeping shortly after the work.

### Why did my T&P valve start leaking after fitting a PRV?

Because the PRV closed the system. Heated water expands, and with the path back to the main shut off, the pressure has nowhere to go — so the relief valve opens exactly as designed. The valve is not faulty. Add or correct the expansion tank.

### Will a PRV fix low water pressure?

No — it can only reduce pressure, never increase it. If the complaint is weak *flow* with healthy static pressure, the cause is friction in the pipe and a PRV makes it worse. Take a static and a flowing reading before touching anything at the meter.

### How long does a pressure reducing valve last?

Typically ten to twenty years. They are a spring and a diaphragm, and they generally fail **low** — producing a gradual whole-house pressure loss with no other explanation. A gauge downstream turns that from a suspicion into a measurement.

### Is 80 psi too high for a house?

It is the point at which the code stops permitting it. Below 80 you are legal, and above about 70 you may already be outside appliance warranties. Nothing about the fixtures improves above roughly 65 psi, so the pressure at the top of the range is doing work on your plumbing rather than for you.

### What else closes a system besides a PRV?

A backflow preventer, a dual check, or a **check valve in the meter assembly** — which many water utilities now fit as standard. That last one is why plenty of houses became closed systems with no work done inside them, and why a heater that behaved for a decade suddenly starts weeping at the relief valve.
