The rule everybody half-remembers is "receptacles every 12 feet." That's the *consequence*, not the rule, and the difference matters because reasoning from the wrong version of it puts outlets in the wrong places.

**What 210.52(A) actually says is that no point along the floor line of any wall space may be more than 6 feet from a receptacle.** It's a statement about reach: the code is making sure a lamp with a 6-foot cord can plug in from anywhere along a wall without an extension cord. The 12-foot spacing falls out of overlapping two 6-foot radii.

Read it as a spacing rule and you'll get the corners wrong — because a receptacle 12 feet from the *last one* can still be more than 6 feet from the end of the wall.

## The Rule, Drawn

On a 20-foot wall, receptacles at 6 ft and 18 ft satisfy the rule exactly:

- The first covers 0–12 ft.
- The second covers 12–20 ft (its reach runs past the corner).
- They're 12 ft apart, which is the maximum.
- Neither is more than 6 ft from a corner.

**The corner is where installations fail.** An outlet may be no more than 6 feet from the end of a wall space, so on a 14-foot wall a single receptacle dead centre works (7 ft either way? no — 7 ft exceeds 6 ft), and actually needs to sit within 6 ft of *both* ends, which on a 14-foot wall means between 6 and 8 ft. On anything over 12 feet you need two.

### What counts as "wall space"

This is the other half of 210.52(A), and it's more generous than people expect. **210.52(A)(2)** defines wall space as:

- Any **unbroken wall space 2 feet or more in width**, including space measured around corners.
- Space occupied by **fixed panels in walls**, excluding sliding-panel doorways.
- Space afforded by **fixed room dividers** such as freestanding bar-type counters or railings.

Three consequences worth knowing:

**A 2-foot section of wall counts.** The short return beside a doorway, or the wall between two windows, is wall space if it's 2 feet wide — and it needs to be within 6 feet of an outlet like anything else.

**You measure around corners.** A 6-foot reach continues around an inside corner, which is why a receptacle near a corner covers both walls.

**Railings and fixed dividers count as wall.** An open-plan room with a half-wall or a railing has wall space along it, which surprises people finishing basements.

**Doorways don't count**, and neither does the space occupied by fireplaces or fixed cabinetry. So a wall largely taken up by a sliding door may need no receptacle at all.

**210.52(A)(3)** also lets a **floor receptacle within 18 inches of the wall** count toward the requirement, which is the standard solution for a long wall of glass.

## Every Rule in 210.52

<BlogInfographic
  title="210.52 is nine unrelated rules with different numbers"
  caption="The 6 ft rule is only subsection (A). The others each have their own trigger."
>
  <SpacingByRoomDiagram />
</BlogInfographic>

| Subsection | Location | Requirement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| (A) | Habitable rooms | No point along the floor line more than 6 ft from a receptacle |
| (A)(2) | What counts as wall | Unbroken wall space 2 ft or wider, plus fixed panels and railings |
| (B) | Kitchen and dining | At least **two** 20 A small-appliance branch circuits |
| (C) | Kitchen countertops | No point more than **24 in** from a receptacle; counters 12 in or wider |
| (D) | Bathrooms | At least one within **3 ft** of the outside edge of each basin |
| (E) | Outdoors | One at the front and one at the back, not over **6½ ft** above grade |
| (F) | Laundry | At least one, on its own 20 A circuit |
| (G) | Garages, basements, accessory buildings | At least one per **vehicle bay**, not over 5½ ft above the floor |
| (H) | Hallways | At least one where the hallway is **10 ft or longer** |
| (I) | Foyers | On walls 3 ft or wider where the foyer exceeds **60 ft²** |

Two things to notice about this list.

**None of these numbers is 6 feet except subsection (A).** Countertops are 24 inches, basins 3 feet, hallways a 10-foot trigger. Carrying the 6-foot rule into a kitchen is the single most common error, and it produces a legal wall and an illegal counter.

**Every one is a minimum.** Nothing in 210.52 caps the number of receptacles anywhere, and nothing in the NEC limits how many you may put on a dwelling branch circuit — that structural point is covered in **[How Many Outlets on a 20 Amp Circuit](/blog/how-many-outlets-on-a-20-amp-circuit)**.

## Kitchen Countertops

<BlogInfographic
  title="Countertops use 24 in, not 6 ft — a fourfold difference"
  caption="An 8 ft counter run. 210.52(C) applies to any counter space 12 in or wider."
>
  <SpacingCounterDiagram />
</BlogInfographic>

**210.52(C)(1)**: a receptacle must be installed so that **no point along the countertop wall line is more than 24 inches** from a receptacle. Which means receptacles at most **48 inches apart**, and within 24 inches of the end of each counter run.

**Any counter space 12 inches or wider** triggers the requirement. That catches the narrow run beside a fridge and the sliver of counter at the end of a peninsula.

**A counter run is broken by** a range, a sink or a refrigerator space, and each resulting section is measured on its own. A 30-inch section of counter between a sink and a range is its own run and needs its own receptacle.

Also required in a kitchen:

- **Two 20 A small-appliance circuits minimum** — 210.52(B) — serving the countertop receptacles, and 210.52(B)(2) generally prohibits those circuits from serving other outlets.
- **GFCI protection** on all kitchen countertop receptacles, and on much else besides. See **[Where GFCI Protection Is Required](/blog/where-gfci-is-required)**.
- **Receptacles not more than 20 inches above the countertop** where they serve it, under 210.52(C)(3).

### Islands and peninsulas — check your code cycle

This is genuinely in flux and worth handling carefully rather than confidently.

**Under NEC 2020**, 210.52(C)(2) required receptacles on island and peninsular countertops by area — at least one for the first 9 ft² and an additional one for each 18 ft² beyond.

**The 2023 cycle removed that requirement.** Island and peninsular countertops no longer require a receptacle; where one *is* installed, the location rules still govern (and 210.52(C)(3) prohibits a face-up installation in a countertop surface).

So the answer depends entirely on which cycle your jurisdiction has adopted, and adoption lags publication by years. **Confirm with the AHJ before designing an island without a receptacle** — and note that a kitchen island with no power is a functional decision as well as a code one, so many designers still put one in. Which cycle applies where is the subject of **[NEC Adoption by State](/blog/nec-adoption-by-state)**.

## The Ones People Forget

**Hallways.** A hallway 10 feet or more in length — measured along the centreline without passing through a doorway — needs at least one receptacle. Easy to miss on a rough-in walkthrough.

**Foyers over 60 ft².** 210.52(I) applies the requirement to walls 3 feet or wider. A generous entryway is not exempt just because it isn't a "room."

**Garage vehicle bays.** 210.52(G)(1) requires at least one receptacle **in each vehicle bay**, not more than 5½ feet above the floor. A two-car garage needs two, and the height limit is there so the outlet isn't behind a wall-mounted cabinet.

**Basements.** At least one receptacle in each separate unfinished portion. Finish the basement and the full 210.52(A) rules apply to the new habitable space.

**Outdoors, front and back.** Both, at grade level, not over 6½ feet above grade. Balconies, decks and porches accessible from inside the dwelling also require one under 210.52(E)(3).

**Above and below the counter is not interchangeable.** A receptacle more than 20 inches above the countertop doesn't count toward the countertop requirement even if it's on the same wall.

## Adjacent Requirements That Travel With Spacing

Getting the layout right is only part of a compliant rough-in:

**AFCI** — 210.12 requires it for most dwelling living-area branch circuits. **GFCI** — 210.8 for kitchens, bathrooms, garages, outdoors, basements, laundry areas and more. The distinction between the two devices is in **[GFCI vs AFCI](/blog/gfci-vs-afci)**.

**Tamper-resistant receptacles** — 406.12 requires them in most dwelling locations.

**Box fill** — every receptacle you add is a device yoke counted **twice** under Table 314.16, and that arithmetic gets tight fast. See **[Box Fill Calculation](/blog/box-fill-calculation)**.

**Circuit count** — the number of receptacles you install doesn't change the load calculation in a dwelling, because 220.14(J) has already counted general-use receptacle outlets inside the 3 VA/ft² general lighting load. So a generous layout costs you devices and boxes, not circuits.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating it as a 12-foot spacing rule.** It's a 6-foot reach rule; 12 ft is what falls out in the middle of a wall.
- **Missing the corner case.** No point may be over 6 ft from an outlet, including the end of the wall.
- **Applying 6 ft to a countertop.** Counters are 24 inches — a fourfold difference.
- **Ignoring 2-foot wall sections.** The short return beside a door is wall space.
- **Forgetting railings and fixed dividers count as wall.** Common miss in finished basements.
- **Measuring a counter run through a sink or range.** Those break the run; each section is measured separately.
- **Assuming islands always need a receptacle.** Required under NEC 2020, not required under 2023. Check the adopted cycle.
- **One receptacle in a two-car garage.** 210.52(G)(1) requires one per vehicle bay.
- **Skipping the 10-foot hallway.** It needs one.
- **Counting a receptacle 24 in above the counter toward the countertop rule.** The limit is 20 inches.

## FAQ

### What is the 6-foot rule for receptacles?

NEC 210.52(A) requires that no point along the floor line of any wall space be more than 6 feet from a receptacle outlet. It's a reach requirement — the intent is that an appliance with a 6-foot cord can plug in from anywhere along a wall. Receptacles ending up about 12 feet apart is a consequence of overlapping two 6-foot reaches, not the rule itself.

### How far apart should outlets be?

At most 12 feet apart in the middle of a wall, and within 6 feet of the end of each wall space. The end-of-wall condition is the one people miss: spacing outlets exactly 12 feet apart from the first one can still leave a corner more than 6 feet from any receptacle.

### Does a 2-foot wall need a receptacle?

The wall space itself must be within 6 feet of a receptacle, and 210.52(A)(2) counts any unbroken wall space 2 feet or wider as wall space. So a 2-foot return beside a doorway doesn't necessarily need its own outlet, but it does have to be within reach of one — and because you measure around corners, a receptacle on the adjacent wall often covers it.

### What is the receptacle spacing for kitchen countertops?

24 inches, not 6 feet. 210.52(C)(1) requires that no point along the countertop wall line be more than 24 inches from a receptacle, which means receptacles at most 48 inches apart. It applies to any counter space 12 inches or wider, and a run is broken by a sink, range or refrigerator space with each section measured separately.

### Do kitchen islands need receptacles?

It depends on your code cycle, and this changed recently. NEC 2020 required them by area — one for the first 9 ft² and one more per additional 18 ft². The 2023 cycle removed the requirement, though location rules still apply where a receptacle is installed. Confirm which cycle your jurisdiction has adopted before designing an island without one.

### How many receptacles does a garage need?

At least one in each vehicle bay, not more than 5½ feet above the floor, under 210.52(G)(1). A two-car garage needs two. The height limit exists so the receptacle isn't rendered inaccessible by wall-mounted storage. Garage receptacles also require GFCI protection under 210.8(A).

### Do hallways need receptacles?

Yes, where the hallway is 10 feet or more in length measured along the centreline without passing through a doorway. 210.52(H) requires at least one. It's one of the most commonly missed items on a rough-in inspection because a hallway doesn't feel like a room with wall space.

### Does installing more receptacles mean I need more circuits?

Not in a dwelling. 220.14(J) includes general-use receptacle outlets in the 3 VA per square foot general lighting load, so the load calculation doesn't change with the number of devices. A generous layout costs you devices, boxes and box-fill volume rather than circuits. In a non-dwelling occupancy it's different — 220.14(I) counts 180 VA per strap.
