The plumbing defects that fail inspections are not exotic. They are a short, well-known list, and the reason they keep happening is not ignorance — it is that **almost none of them look wrong.**

A trap arm twelve feet long looks exactly like one eight feet long. A drain at 1/8 inch per foot looks exactly like one at 1/4. You cannot see any of it from the doorway, which is why it survives the work and surfaces at the inspection.

## The list

<BlogInfographic
  title="Almost none of these look wrong"

**Trap arm too long.** A 2 inch arm stops at **8 ft**, a 1-1/2 inch at **6 ft**, measured weir to vent fitting. Relocating a fixture during a remodel is how it happens, and it is the most common single failure. Why the limit exists is in **[why is my drain gurgling](/blog/why-is-my-drain-gurgling)**.

**Drain slope wrong.** **1/4 inch per foot** at 2 inch and under — 2.08%. Too flat and solids sit; too steep and the water outruns them. Both directions fail.

**An S-trap.** Prohibited outright, and no length of arm makes it legal — it siphons its own seal by design. Still common in older work and in DIY vanity swaps.

**Building drain undersized where a water closet discharges.** Never under **3 inch**, regardless of what the fixture unit table says. A floor that overrides the arithmetic entirely — see **[what size drain pipe do I need](/blog/what-size-drain-pipe-do-i-need)**.

**Vent undersized.** Half the drain diameter, never under 1-1/4 inch, and **upsized past 40 ft of developed length**. The length is the part that gets missed.

**No air gap on a discharge.** **1.00 inch** minimum on a 1/2 inch opening, 1.50 near a wall. Dishwasher and water softener discharges are the usual finds — the rule is in **[backflow prevention](/blog/backflow-prevention-explained)**.

**No PRV, or a PRV with no expansion tank.** A PRV is required above **80 psi**, and fitting one creates a closed system that then requires expansion control. The second requirement is the one that gets forgotten — **[do I need a PRV](/blog/do-i-need-a-pressure-reducing-valve)**.

## Why timing is the whole cost

The same two-foot-too-long trap arm costs an hour during rough-in and a great deal more once there are finishes over it. **The plumbing repair is identical in every row** — what changes is what has to be destroyed to reach it, and by the time tile and fixtures are in, that dominates completely.

That is the entire argument for the rough-in inspection being the cheapest hour anyone spends on a job, and for nobody rushing it.

It is also the argument for building discovery risk into estimates honestly rather than optimistically — **[how to estimate plumbing jobs](/blog/how-to-estimate-plumbing-jobs)** covers where that belongs in a price.

## The walk-round

<BlogInfographic
  title="The walk-round before you call it in"
  caption="Expressed as things to measure rather than things to know, because nearly every common failure is dimensional. Three of the four gauge checks take a minute each."
>
  <ViolationsChecklistDiagram />
</BlogInfographic>

**Take a tape to** every trap arm, the slope on every horizontal, the developed length of every vent, and every air gap.

**Take a gauge to** static pressure, the expansion tank pre-charge if the system is closed, the test pressure the AHJ wants held, and the delivered hot water temperature.

**Just look for** S-traps, cleanouts that exist but are not accessible, missing nail plates where pipe crosses framing, supports at the right spacing, and dielectric unions between dissimilar metals.

Two habits worth more than the list:

**Ask the inspector what they check first.** Every one of them has an order and most will tell you. It costs a phone call and it is the highest-return conversation available on the job.

**Confirm which code and which edition.** Everything above is IPC, and roughly fifteen states enforce the UPC or a derivative — with different trap arm measurement, different fixture unit values and a different position on air admittance valves. **[IPC vs UPC](/blog/ipc-vs-upc-differences)** sets out exactly which of these numbers change.

## The permit question

Unpermitted plumbing is a slower failure than a failed inspection and a worse one. It surfaces at resale, when a buyer's inspector or an appraiser finds work with no record, and it can complicate an insurance claim on water damage.

The general position is that a permit is required for anything beyond minor repairs and like-for-like replacement of a fixture — but **the actual threshold is local**, and the range between jurisdictions is wide. Ask before assuming.

## Frequently asked questions

### What are the most common plumbing code violations?

Trap arms exceeding their length limit, drain slope outside the required range, S-traps, building drains undersized where a water closet discharges, undersized or under-length-corrected vents, missing air gaps on appliance discharges, and a pressure reducing valve fitted without the expansion tank it requires.

### Why do inspectors catch things that look fine?

Because almost every common failure is **dimensional rather than visible**. A 12 ft trap arm looks identical to an 8 ft one, and a drain at 1/8 inch per foot looks identical to one at 1/4. Five of the seven most common defects need a tape measure or a gauge to find at all.

### How far can a trap be from its vent?

**8 ft for a 2 inch arm, 6 ft for 1-1/2 inch, 5 ft for 1-1/4 inch**, measured from the trap weir to the vent fitting under IPC Table 909.1. Those limits correspond to one pipe diameter of fall, past which the vent opening drops below the trap weir.

### What slope does a drain need?

**1/4 inch per foot — 2.08% — for 2-1/2 inch and under**, 1/8 inch per foot for 3 to 6 inch, and 1/16 above that. Both directions fail: too flat and solids settle, too steep and the water runs away from them.

### Why are S-traps not allowed?

Because they siphon their own seal. The vertical drop immediately after the trap creates the siphon that empties it, and no length of arm fixes it — which is why the prohibition is outright rather than dimensional. A P-trap with a properly located vent is the replacement.

### What does it cost to fix a code violation after the fact?

Almost entirely a function of when it is found. The same trap arm correction is roughly an hour's work during rough-in, and many times that after tile and fixtures — the plumbing repair is identical, and what changes is what has to be demolished and rebuilt to reach it.

### Do I need a permit for plumbing work?

For most work beyond minor repairs and like-for-like fixture replacement, yes — but **the threshold is set locally** and varies widely. Unpermitted work surfaces at resale and can complicate a water damage insurance claim, which makes it a slower and worse failure than a failed inspection.

### Are these rules the same everywhere?

No. Everything here is IPC, and roughly fifteen states enforce the UPC or a derivative, which measures trap arms differently, uses its own fixture unit values, and takes a different position on air admittance valves. Confirm the code, the edition and the local amendments your jurisdiction has actually adopted.
